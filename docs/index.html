It’s really hard to beat the ECR - 7/27

What I learned trying to out-rank FantasyPros with 53 features and 978 player-seasons: the experts are already very good, and eight seasons is not enough to prove otherwise.

12-team PPR redraft · test seasons 2018–2025 · graded on realized value over replacement · every number on this page reproduces from bff.methodology_audit

I built a preseason draft-ranking model, backtested it properly against the expert consensus, and it does not beat the expert consensus. That part is mildly disappointing and completely ordinary. What turned out to be interesting is why, and the fact that the usual way of checking (backtest a few seasons, look at the average, declare victory) cannot actually tell the difference between a real edge and a lucky one. Below is the whole thing, including the experiment that convinced me to stop.

How the model works

It does not build a board from scratch. It starts from the FantasyPros expert consensus rank (ECR) and tries to correct it, which is a much easier job than ranking 150 players from nothing.

Three steps. One: turn each player’s expert rank into expected points, using history (what does the 5th-ranked WR usually score? the 30th?). Two: predict the miss. This is the model proper, and the input is 53 things knowable in August: age and the shape of the aging curve at each position, how much a player outscored or undershot his draft price last year, games missed, injury history, targets and carries vacated by teammates who left, rookie draft capital, contract year, whether his quarterback changed, and so on. Three: convert points into value over replacement, because raw points lie across positions; the 10th-best QB outscores the 10th-best TE but is worth less, since a decent QB is always available.

Step two is a ridge regression, and the choice matters for everything that follows. With 53 inputs and only 978 training rows (really only 368 distinct players, since a player shows up in several seasons), an ordinary regression would happily memorize noise: it would “discover” that 27-year-old tight ends on new teams bust, because three of them did. Ridge is the standard defence. It fits the regression while paying a penalty for large coefficients, so every effect gets pulled toward zero unless the data insists. One dial (alpha) sets how hard it pulls. This model then adds a second dial: only 30% of whatever correction survives is actually applied to the expert baseline. Both dials are chosen on an early block of seasons and then frozen before the test seasons are ever scored.

Where the dials landed is itself a result: alpha = 300 is the strongest setting on the grid and shrink = 0.3 the weakest. Of 53 inputs, that leaves about 9.5 inputs’ worth of real fitting. The search is turning the model down as far as it is allowed to.

The scoreboard

Champion versus challenger, one round per season. Three boards are fixed before Week 1 (this model, the expert consensus, and the market’s average draft position), then graded after the season on how well the preseason order matched what players actually returned in value over replacement. Same grade for everyone, no board sees the season it predicts.

Table 1. Model vs FantasyPros ECR, per season. Higher is better; 1.0 would be a perfect ordering, 0.0 a coin flip.

So: beat the market in 7 of 8 seasons (0.5237 vs 0.5040), and 4 of 8 against the experts (0.5237 vs 0.5205). Beating ADP is real. Against ECR it is a coin flip, and the right word for a coin flip is “tied”.

For scale, in football terms: this board puts about 49% of the eventual top-24 finishers in its own top 24. So does the expert list. Preseason ranking is a genuinely hard forecasting problem for everyone involved.

Are the experts actually better than the market?

The model is built on top of ECR, so it inherits whatever edge ECR has. That makes one question worth asking on its own, with no model involved: graded on realized value, is the expert consensus a better preseason board than where people actually draft? The answer depends sharply on the era.

Here the experts pull clearly ahead: ECR wins 7 of 8 seasons by a mean of +0.0165, and the margin grows over time. Whether that is FantasyPros getting better, the ADP sample thinning out, or both, this data cannot settle.

What that implies about my model, and it is not flattering. On the test seasons the model beats ADP by +0.0197. But ECR on its own, with no model attached, already beats ADP by +0.0165 over those same seasons. The regression contributes the remaining +0.0032. So roughly five sixths of my margin over the market is the expert list I started from, not the corrections I added.

It also resolves a puzzle. The model shows no edge at all on its own tuning seasons (0.4293 against ADP’s 0.4305) yet +0.0197 on test. Not a contradiction: in the tuning years the expert anchor was worth nothing over ADP, so a model standing on it had nothing inherited to win with. In the test years the anchor was worth +0.0165 before the model did anything at all. What changed between windows is not my model; it is what my model is standing on.

Is a fantasy season just noise?

The obvious excuse for a mediocre score is that the outcome is random, so nobody could do better. I checked, and it is not true.

Split each season into odd and even weeks and rank the same players twice. If seasons were mostly luck the two halves would disagree. They agree at 0.81, which corrects to about 0.89 for a full season. In other words the thing being graded is measured well, and the distance between a 0.52 and a perfect board is real forecasting error, not scoring slop. There is genuine headroom up there. Nobody, expert or model, is reaching it.

Reliability by position: QB 0.77, RB 0.91, WR 0.85, TE 0.77. This is an upper bound, incidentally: a season-ending injury looks “consistent” across both halves even though it was unforecastable in August.

Why the experts are so hard to beat

Here is the number that reframed the project for me. Take my features and use them to predict the expert consensus itself instead of the season outcome. They explain 65% of it.

That uses 52 of the 53. One feature is excluded on purpose: it is built from the expert rank itself (last season’s points per game minus what the expert rank implied), so letting it predict the expert rank would be circular. Including it would read 69% instead.

Which makes sense once you say it out loud. ECR is roughly a hundred analysts reading the same box scores, the same depth charts, the same ages and the same beat reports I am feeding my regression. Age curves and vacated targets and rookie draft capital are not secrets. So two-thirds of my “model” is a reconstruction of work the experts already did, and only the leftover third is even eligible to be an edge.

And there is nothing in the leftovers

That leftover third is exactly what the regression is trained on: not “how many points will he score” but “where are the experts wrong”. So I measured whether it gets that right on seasons it had never seen, fold by fold.

The correlation between the predicted miss and the actual miss is 0.070. Not 0.3, not 0.15. About 0.07, and negative in 2 of the 5 seasons tested. Measured as variance explained it is essentially zero (-0.0028, i.e. very slightly worse than predicting nothing). In sample it looks like it is working, explaining 11% of the miss; out of sample that evaporates. Which is the whole reason to insist on out-of-sample testing, and also why the tuning dials ended up pinned at “trust this as little as possible”.

The experiment that convinced me: scrambled data

This is the one to remember if you take nothing else from the post.

I took the training data and shuffled the outcomes within each season, so that a player’s features were attached to a random other player’s result. Every real relationship destroyed on purpose. Then I ran the identical pipeline: same ridge, same dials, same value conversion, same grading.

Three scrambled runs scored 0.4269, 0.4350, 0.4206. My real model scores 0.4293. Doing nothing at all, just ranking by the expert anchor with no regression, scores 0.4306.

One of the scrambled runs beat my real model by +0.0057, which is larger than the entire edge over ECR I would otherwise be claiming (+0.0032). Read that as the measuring instrument rattling: pure noise fed through this pipeline lands in the same neighbourhood as real signal, so the neighbourhood is where the argument dies. Any “improvement” smaller than that rattle is unprovable, and nearly every improvement anyone reports in this hobby is smaller than that rattle.

Even a real edge would be invisible

Suppose the model really is +0.0032 better than the experts. Could this test see it? No. Season-to-season the gap bounces around with a spread of 0.0114, so with 8 seasons the smallest edge detectable at conventional standards is about +0.0112, three times the effect. To reliably confirm an edge this size I would need roughly 82 seasons of NFL history graded the same way. I have eight, and I get one more per year.

The same arithmetic undercuts my nicer-looking result. Beating ADP by +0.0197 in 7 of 8 seasons gives a one-sided p-value of 0.0469, which looks publishable, except that the smallest edge this design can detect is +0.0268. When a test clears the bar on an effect it was not powerful enough to find, that is a lucky draw more than a demonstration.

And it poisoned my feature selection. My rule for keeping a new group of features was that it had to improve the validation score by +0.0020. But changing a single setting moves that score by 0.0040 to 0.0083 run to run. The bar was smaller than the wobble, which means every keep-or-drop call I made was roughly a coin flip. Two of them can be checked, because I later scored them on the test seasons: a player-trajectory block passed validation at +0.0059 and delivered +0.0003, and a wider hyperparameter search passed at +0.0068 and delivered −0.0037. Two for two in the wrong direction. That is what coin flips look like.

One thing I did find genuinely wrong

Not everything here is a null result. Auditing the loss function turned up a real design flaw.

The model is graded on getting the order right, but it is fit by minimizing squared error on points. Those are not the same target, and here is the damage: the 11.5% of players who got hurt or busted (under 50 points on the season) account for 72.5% of the total error the regression is trying to reduce. So the model spends nearly three-quarters of its effort trying to predict which stars will tear an ACL, which nobody can do, and a quarter on ordering the healthy middle of the board, which is the actual job. There is a safety valve meant to contain this, capping any single player’s error at 4 on the log scale, but it only ever triggers on 1.4% of players; it is set so loose it does nothing.

Tightening that cap helps, and monotonically: 0.4293 → 0.4354 → 0.4378 → 0.4397 as the cap goes 4, 2, 1, 0.5. The tightest setting is the first version of this model that beats the experts, the market, and doing-nothing simultaneously on the validation window. But I am not claiming it, because I just spent this whole post explaining why numbers that size are not claimable: 4 of 5 seasons improve, one season supplies most of the gain, and holding the other dials fixed the cap alone is worth +0.0025. It is a good hypothesis with a mechanism behind it. It is not a result.

So what actually helps your draft?

Three things I would still defend, none of which are “I have better player opinions than the experts”.

1. Convert to value over replacement, not points. This is where nearly all of the real movement on my board comes from, and it is a modelling choice rather than a prediction. A tight end who scores 15 fewer points than a quarterback can be worth much more, because the quarterback you would otherwise stream is nearly as good and the tight end you would otherwise stream is not. Getting the exchange rate right between positions beats getting player 43 versus player 47 right.

2. Draft timing is a separate question from value. Who is worth the most and who you should take now are different, because positions dry up at different speeds. That is where running-back scarcity legitimately lives, and it is on the draft page rather than in the rankings.

3. Treat any model board, including mine, as the expert consensus with a tilt. That is what it is, measurably. The 2026 board is public, every player row shows exactly which features moved him and by how much, and if a tilt looks stupid to you, it probably is; the evidence that it knows better than you is not there.

One more measurement worth stealing if you do this yourself. Comparing two boards by their season-average correlation throws away almost everything: a whole season of ~150 players collapses into one number, and then you have eight numbers. Compare them per player instead, asking how many ranks each board missed by. On my validation seasons that is 33.1 ranks of average miss for the model against 33.0 for the experts across 743 player-seasons, with the difference pinned inside about a rank ([-0.69, +0.91]). Same conclusion, but it can see effects the season-average version never will.

Method, and what to distrust

Value over replacement uses QB8 / RB30 / WR36 / TE8 (QB and TE streaming-aware). Predictions for season t use only seasons before t plus season-t preseason facts: expert ranks, draft position, rosters, the April draft. No leakage, and the scrambled-data test above doubles as a check on that.

    The honest caveats: I have looked at the test seasons more than a pre-registered study would allow, so treat the p-values as descriptive. The value conversion is fixed for 12-team one-quarterback PPR and would move in other formats. Some rookies run on partly empty opportunity features. And the residual cap discussed above is still set where it was, not where the audit suggests.
